What is Noema?

You photograph your handwritten linear algebra. Noema finds the first step that is actually wrong, then animates your claimed result applied to the plane — the grid and the basis vectors — right beside what the problem asked for.

It never tells you the answer. It points at the step and lets you watch the two pictures come apart.

Why not just mark it wrong

A red cross tells you that something failed. It does not tell you what you were picturing incorrectly. Most linear algebra mistakes are geometric: a transformation applied in the wrong order, a basis vector landing somewhere it shouldn't, a projection that fails to meet its target at a right angle. Those are shapes, not numbers — so Noema shows you the shape.

How it works

  1. Read. A vision model transcribes the page as a transcriber, not a solver. It returns typed steps — raw text, parsed value, confidence, ambiguities, crossed-out flags — and is forbidden to fix anything it sees. You can correct anything the camera misread. If you would rather type than photograph, write one step per line (AB = [0 -3; 1 0]) and the same checks run on that instead.
  2. Verify. Each step is checked with sympy, not a language model. The check runs against the original given data, never against Noema's own solution path — because your route through a problem is legitimately allowed to differ from anyone else's.
  3. Locate. Only the first failing step is blamed. Later arithmetic that faithfully carries a bad number forward is not your mistake, so it is not marked as one.
  4. Animate. A scene template is chosen and filled with numbers computed from the verification — the model never writes the animation. Your values drive one panel; the problem's requirements drive the other.
  5. Point. A one-line hint names a location and a thing to watch.

What it will not do

The reference panel is masked on purpose. Where the correct matrix, determinant, eigenvalue or solution would be printed, you see [? ?], and the heading reads “what the problem asks for” — never “what it should be”. The geometry is the whole message.

The hint is held to the same rule by a linter that rejects digits, spelled-out numbers, fractions, percentages, and even the difference or ratio between your value and the right one. A hint reads like watch where the first basis vector lands in your step 2 — a place to look, never the correction.

It gives you the benefit of the doubt

A step counts as wrong only if every plausible reading of it, against every plausible reading of the givens, is wrong. Handwriting that cannot be parsed is not treated as a mistake. Work you crossed out yourself is never held against you. And writing 0.71 for 1/√2 is a rounding, not a linear algebra error.
